How have house prices in Liverpool and Newcastle upon Tyne performed over the last 25 years? Both cities have experienced significant appreciation, but the scale and timing of that growth have differed.

Using **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, we compared annual average recorded sale prices from 2000 to 2025, examining long-term appreciation, the financial crisis, volatility and the changing price gap.

**Liverpool's average recorded sale price increased by 290.48%**, compared with **241.70% in Newcastle**.

Newcastle remained more expensive throughout the period, but its percentage premium over Liverpool narrowed considerably.

## Liverpool vs Newcastle: key statistics

| Measure | Liverpool | Newcastle upon Tyne |
|---|---:|---:|
| Average price, 2000 | £61,374 | £75,897 |
| Average price, 2015 | £151,978 | £189,916 |
| Average price, 2020 | £185,832 | £225,645 |
| Average price, 2025 | £239,652 | £259,343 |
| Growth, 2000–2025 | 290.48% | 241.70% |
| Growth, 2015–2025 | 57.69% | 36.56% |
| Annualised growth | 5.60% | 5.04% |
| Annual growth volatility | 7.23% | 7.35% |

| Recorded sales, 2000–2025 | 234,078 | 147,942 |

These figures are based on annual arithmetic means of recorded transactions, rather than valuations of an unchanged collection of properties.

## How have average house prices changed?

<img src="/images/article-40/liverpool-vs-newcastle-average-house-prices.png" alt="Line chart comparing average recorded house prices in Liverpool and Newcastle upon Tyne between 2000 and 2025" loading="lazy" />

Liverpool's average recorded sale price increased from **£61,374 in 2000** to **£239,652 in 2025**.

Newcastle started at **£75,897** and reached **£259,343**.

Newcastle had the higher average in **all 26 years**. Yet the distance between the two markets was far from constant.

The difference in 2000 was **£14,523**. By 2025, it was **£19,690**.

Both cities experienced rising prices over the full period, but their annual averages also reveal temporary setbacks and changing relative performance.

An important qualification is that the mix of homes sold can vary considerably between years. For example, a higher proportion of detached-house transactions can raise an annual average even without an equivalent increase in the value of individual properties.

## Which city delivered stronger capital growth?

<img src="/images/article-40/liverpool-vs-newcastle-indexed-growth.png" alt="Indexed house-price growth in Liverpool and Newcastle from 2000 to 2025 with both cities starting at 100" loading="lazy" />

Absolute prices alone do not tell us which city appreciated more rapidly. Newcastle began with a higher average, so we also calculated an index with **2000 equal to 100** for each city.

By 2025, Liverpool's index reached **390.5**, while Newcastle's reached **341.7**.

This represents **290.48% cumulative nominal growth in Liverpool** versus **241.70% in Newcastle**.

The compound annual growth rates were **5.60%** and **5.04%**, respectively.

Liverpool therefore achieved stronger historical percentage appreciation across the full 25-year interval.

However, these are nominal changes in transaction averages. They are not inflation-adjusted, constant-quality house-price indices or investment returns after financing and other costs.

## What happened during the last decade?

Looking at 2015–2025 helps establish whether Liverpool's outperformance was confined to the earlier years.

Liverpool's average increased from **£151,978** to **£239,652**, representing **57.69%** growth.

Newcastle's average increased from **£189,916** to **£259,343**, representing **36.56%** growth.

Liverpool's lead during this decade was **21.13 percentage points**.

This is particularly relevant because the comparison covers a period of changing mortgage conditions, pandemic-era market activity and subsequent borrowing-cost pressures.

Nevertheless, choosing different start and end dates can produce different rankings. Investors should not extrapolate a decade's performance into a forecast for the next decade.

## Has Newcastle become cheaper relative to Liverpool?

<img src="/images/article-40/newcastle-price-premium-over-liverpool.png" alt="Newcastle upon Tyne average house-price premium relative to Liverpool from 2000 to 2025" loading="lazy" />

The third graph measures Newcastle's price premium as a percentage of Liverpool's annual average.

In 2000, Newcastle's average was **23.66% higher** than Liverpool's.

By 2015, the premium stood at **24.96%**.

In 2025, it had narrowed to **8.22%**.

This means Liverpool has moved closer to Newcastle in relative price terms, even though Newcastle remained the more expensive city every year.

The distinction between percentage and absolute gaps matters. In 2025, Newcastle still cost **£19,690 more on average**, despite its much smaller percentage premium.

The narrowing premium is a useful historical observation, not a guarantee that Liverpool will eventually overtake Newcastle.

## How did the financial crisis affect both cities?

Both cities experienced declines in annual average recorded sale prices around the period of the global financial crisis.

Liverpool's maximum observed drawdown was **-4.61%**, measured from its **2007** annual-average peak to its **2011** trough.

Newcastle's maximum observed drawdown was larger, at **-7.19%**, between **2008** and **2011**.

Liverpool regained its previous nominal annual-average peak in **2014**, while Newcastle did so in **2015**.

These observations suggest that Newcastle's recorded annual averages suffered a deeper downturn and took longer to recover in this dataset.

However, a maximum drawdown calculated from annual citywide transaction averages does not capture every interim fluctuation or the actual sale price of a specific property.

## Which city's prices were more volatile?

We calculated annual percentage changes and measured their sample standard deviation.

Liverpool's annual growth volatility was **7.23 percentage points**.

Newcastle's was **7.35 percentage points**.

The figures were relatively close, although Newcastle exhibited slightly greater annual variation.

The correlation between the cities' annual percentage changes was **+0.763**, indicating a positive historical relationship.

This suggests that broad economic conditions influenced both markets, but it does not mean that they moved identically or that their future performance will be closely linked.

A positive correlation is also not proof of causation between the two cities.

## Which city offers better property investment opportunities?

On the measures available here, Liverpool has several historical advantages: stronger cumulative percentage growth, stronger growth since 2015 and a shallower observed drawdown.

Liverpool also had the lower average recorded sale price in 2025, potentially reducing the cash deposit needed for an otherwise comparable purchase at the same loan-to-value ratio.

But **historical house-price growth is not the same as buy-to-let profitability**.

An investor needs to examine achievable rents, mortgage rates, maintenance, service charges where applicable, insurance, letting fees, taxation and likely vacancy periods.

Property type and postcode also matter. The performance of a particular terrace, flat or family house can differ sharply from the average for its Town/City label.

Our dataset does not include rents, net yields or running costs. It therefore cannot establish which city provides the higher total investment return.

For a meaningful investment comparison, the next step is to analyse individual neighbourhoods and realistic purchase-and-cash-flow scenarios.

## Data source and methodology

This analysis uses **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, grouped by the Town/City labels **LIVERPOOL** and **NEWCASTLE UPON TYNE**, for 2000–2025 inclusive.

Annual average prices are arithmetic means of recorded sale prices. Growth is calculated from the relevant annual averages, and compound annual growth uses the number of elapsed years.

Volatility is the sample standard deviation of annual percentage changes. Maximum drawdown is the largest percentage decline from a previous annual-average peak. The Newcastle premium equals Newcastle's average divided by Liverpool's average, minus one.

**Important limitations:** Prices are nominal, not inflation-adjusted. The dataset is not a constant-quality index, and differences in the properties sold each year can influence the averages. Town/City labels do not necessarily match administrative boundaries.

Historical performance does not predict future property returns.
