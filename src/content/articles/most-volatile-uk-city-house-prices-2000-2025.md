Property investors often focus on capital growth. But how much do average house prices fluctuate from year to year, and do cities with larger fluctuations deliver stronger long-term growth?

We analysed **26 years of HM Land Registry Price Paid Data** across 20 cities in England and Wales. The study compares annual changes in average recorded sale prices between 2001 and 2025.

**Bradford was the most volatile city**, with a standard deviation of **7.39 percentage points**. **Reading was the least volatile**, at **4.82 percentage points**.

These are fluctuations in annual average transaction prices, not directly measured changes in the market value of identical homes.

The median volatility across the 20 cities was **6.58 percentage points**.

## Does higher volatility mean higher growth?

<img src="/images/article-29/city-house-price-volatility-vs-growth.png" alt="Scatter plot comparing annualised house-price growth and historical volatility for 20 cities" loading="lazy" />

Across the 20 cities, the Pearson correlation between volatility and annualised growth was **+0.466**.

That is a moderate positive association within this particular sample: cities with greater historical fluctuations tended, on average, to show somewhat stronger long-term price growth.

However, the relationship is far from perfect. Correlation does not establish causation, and 20 city-level observations are not sufficient to establish a general investment rule.

**Manchester recorded the highest annualised growth**, at **6.38%** between 2000 and 2025.

The median annualised growth rate was **5.44%**.

Annualised growth is calculated from the first and last average prices in the series. It is not an average of the annual percentage changes.

## When did house prices fluctuate most?

The heatmap shows the annual percentage change in each city's recorded average sale price. Green indicates an increase and red indicates a decrease.

It makes it easier to identify periods when multiple cities experienced large changes simultaneously, as well as years when individual cities behaved differently.

Because the measure uses annual averages, a sudden change may reflect both genuine market movements and changes in the types or locations of properties sold.

## What does volatility mean for investors?

For property investors, historical price volatility is one part of risk analysis. A market with less variation in average sale prices is not automatically safer, nor is a more volatile city necessarily a better opportunity.

Cash flow, mortgage affordability, rental demand, void periods, maintenance and transaction costs may matter more to an individual investment.

Investors should also distinguish short-term price fluctuations from the ability to sell a particular property quickly at a realistic price.

## Methodology and limitations

- **Source:** HM Land Registry Price Paid Data.
- **Geography:** 20 named Town/City labels in England and Wales.
- **Price series:** Annual arithmetic mean of recorded transaction prices, 2000–2025.
- **Annual change:** (Current-year average / previous-year average − 1) × 100.
- **Volatility:** Sample standard deviation of annual changes, 2001–2025 (25 observations).
- **Annualised growth:** (2025 average / 2000 average)^(1/25) − 1.
- **Correlation:** Pearson correlation across the 20 city observations.

The figures are nominal and not inflation-adjusted. They are not constant-quality house-price indices.

Changes in the mix of property types, sizes, locations and transaction volumes can affect the annual means. The Town/City labels do not necessarily correspond to administrative boundaries.

Volatility in this article is therefore a descriptive statistic for recorded average transaction prices, rather than a direct measure of the investment risk of an individual property.
