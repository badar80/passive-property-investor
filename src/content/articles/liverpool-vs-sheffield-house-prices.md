Liverpool and Sheffield are two major northern English housing markets. Both have experienced substantial house-price growth since 2000, but which has performed better for property investors?

We analysed **26 years of HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, comparing annual average recorded sale prices, percentage growth, historical price gaps and volatility.

The headline finding is unusually close: **Sheffield recorded 293.38% nominal growth between 2000 and 2025, compared with 290.48% in Liverpool.** Liverpool, however, recorded stronger growth over the latest decade.

## Liverpool vs Sheffield: key statistics

| Measure | Liverpool | Sheffield |
|---|---:|---:|
| Average price, 2000 | £61,374 | £66,740 |
| Average price, 2015 | £151,978 | £169,012 |
| Average price, 2025 | £239,652 | £262,543 |
| Growth, 2000–2025 | 290.48% | 293.38% |
| Growth, 2015–2025 | 57.69% | 55.34% |
| Annualised growth, 2000–2025 | 5.60% | 5.63% |
| Annual growth volatility | 7.23% | 6.67% |
| Largest observed drawdown | -4.61% | -4.98% |
| Recorded sales, 2000–2025 | 234,078 | 224,316 |

These are arithmetic averages of recorded transactions, not valuations of identical properties over time.

## How have Liverpool and Sheffield house prices changed?

<img src="/images/article-38/liverpool-vs-sheffield-average-house-prices.png" alt="Line chart of Liverpool and Sheffield annual average house prices between 2000 and 2025" loading="lazy" />

In 2000, Liverpool's average recorded sale price was **£61,374**, compared with **£66,740** in Sheffield.

By 2025, Liverpool had reached **£239,652**, while Sheffield had reached **£262,543**.

Sheffield had the higher annual average in **26 of the 26 years**. Liverpool was higher in **0 years**.

Although both markets became considerably more expensive, the gap was not constant. Annual averages also reflect changes in the types and locations of properties being sold.

## Which city delivered stronger long-term growth?

<img src="/images/article-38/liverpool-vs-sheffield-indexed-growth.png" alt="Indexed house-price growth for Liverpool and Sheffield with 2000 equal to 100" loading="lazy" />

Comparing absolute prices alone can obscure performance because Sheffield began the period with a higher average price.

To address this, the second graph sets both cities' average prices to **100 in 2000**.

By 2025, Liverpool's index stood at **390.5**, while Sheffield's stood at **393.4**.

Over the full 25-year interval, Sheffield achieved **293.38% growth**, narrowly ahead of Liverpool's **290.48%**.

The compound annual growth rates were **5.63% for Sheffield** and **5.60% for Liverpool**.

The difference is small. It would be misleading to describe one city as dramatically outperforming the other on this historical measure.

## What happened between 2015 and 2025?

The result changes when we examine the most recent decade.

Liverpool's average price increased from **£151,978** in 2015 to **£239,652** in 2025, representing **57.69% growth**.

Sheffield rose from **£169,012** to **£262,543**, an increase of **55.34%**.

Liverpool therefore outperformed Sheffield over 2015–2025, despite trailing very slightly over the full 2000–2025 period.

This demonstrates why investors should compare several holding periods. Selecting only one starting year can change which market appears strongest.

Neither result guarantees that future buyers will achieve comparable capital appreciation.

## How much more expensive is Sheffield than Liverpool?

<img src="/images/article-38/sheffield-price-premium-over-liverpool.png" alt="Percentage premium of Sheffield average house prices over Liverpool from 2000 to 2025" loading="lazy" />

In 2000, Sheffield's average recorded sale price was **8.74% higher** than Liverpool's. The difference was **£5,366**.

By 2025, Sheffield's premium was **9.55%**, equivalent to **£22,891**.

The percentage premium at the end of the period was therefore slightly higher than at the beginning, while the difference measured in pounds had expanded considerably.

The graph reveals that this premium fluctuated rather than rising steadily. The relative affordability of the two markets changed substantially within the 25-year period.

An investor comparing entry prices should therefore examine the current market rather than relying on a long-standing reputation for affordability.

## Which market experienced greater price volatility?

We calculated the sample standard deviation of annual percentage changes in average recorded sale prices.

Liverpool's annual growth volatility was **7.23 percentage points**, compared with **6.67 percentage points** for Sheffield.

On this measure, Liverpool's annual price growth was more variable.

However, volatility should not be confused with the largest historical decline.

Liverpool's maximum observed drawdown was **-4.61%**, while Sheffield's was **-4.98%**.

Sheffield's largest drawdown was slightly deeper, even though its overall annual growth volatility was lower.

These statistics describe annual average transaction prices, so they should not be interpreted as the maximum loss an individual property owner would necessarily have experienced.

## How did Liverpool and Sheffield recover from weaker markets?

Liverpool's largest observed decline ran from **2007 to 2011**, with its earlier annual-average peak regained in **2014**.

Sheffield's largest observed decline ran from **2010 to 2011**, with recovery to the previous peak in **2014**.

Both cities therefore regained their earlier nominal annual-average peaks in 2014, despite reaching their respective troughs in different years.

For landlords, periods of weaker capital growth underline the importance of sustainable mortgage payments, maintenance reserves and sufficient cash flow to avoid forced sales.

A property investment can face financial pressure even when its long-term capital appreciation ultimately proves positive.

## Do Liverpool and Sheffield house prices move together?

The Pearson correlation between the two cities' annual percentage price changes was **+0.838**.

This represents a strong positive historical association. Years with relatively strong growth in one city often coincided with stronger growth in the other.

However, their annual movements were not identical.

Different housing stock, transaction volumes and local economic conditions can influence the average sale price recorded in each market.

Correlation does not establish causation or guarantee that both cities will behave similarly in future downturns.

## Liverpool or Sheffield: which is better for property investment?

**Sheffield narrowly leads on 25-year nominal percentage growth**, while **Liverpool leads over the latest decade**.

Liverpool also had the lower average recorded sale price in 2025. For buyers targeting a particular loan-to-value ratio, a lower purchase price may reduce the absolute deposit required.

However, this comparison cannot establish which city offers the better buy-to-let investment.

A proper investment assessment must consider achievable rent, net rental yield, financing, taxation, insurance, repairs, void periods, management costs and local demand.

Two properties with similar purchase prices can generate very different returns depending on their location, condition and tenancy arrangements.

Investors should therefore treat historical city averages as an initial screening tool, not a substitute for analysing individual properties and realistic cash-flow projections.

## Data source and methodology

- **Source:** HM Land Registry Price Paid Data.
- **Geography:** Liverpool and Sheffield Town/City labels.
- **Period:** 2000–2025 inclusive.
- **Average price:** Arithmetic mean of recorded transactions in each year.
- **Total growth:** Percentage change between annual-average endpoint prices.
- **CAGR:** Compound annual growth rate over the elapsed years.
- **Volatility:** Sample standard deviation of annual percentage changes.
- **Maximum drawdown:** Largest decline from an earlier annual-average price peak.
- **Sheffield premium:** Sheffield average divided by Liverpool average, minus one, expressed as a percentage.

Prices are **nominal and not inflation-adjusted**. The dataset is not a constant-quality house-price index. Changes in the mix of properties sold can influence average prices.

Town/City labels do not necessarily match local-authority boundaries. The analysis does not include rental income, operating expenses or transaction costs.
