How much have house prices in Nottingham increased since 2000? What happened during the financial crisis, and how quickly did prices recover?

We analysed **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data** to examine annual average recorded property sale prices and transaction volumes in Nottingham from 2000 through 2025.

The average recorded sale price increased from **£72,456 in 2000** to **£279,446 in 2025**.

That represents **285.68% nominal growth** over 25 years.

However, the long-term increase conceals considerable variation between years, including a decline during the financial crisis and slower growth more recently.

Our three original graphs examine the price trajectory, annual percentage changes and completed property transactions.

## How have Nottingham house prices changed since 2000?

The first graph shows the long-term trend in Nottingham's average recorded sale prices.

In 2000, the average was **£72,456**.

By 2005, it had increased to **£146,561**, following a period of particularly strong appreciation.

In 2007, the average reached **£154,364**.

The financial crisis interrupted this trend, and the average fell in 2008.

By 2010, the average stood at **£152,982**, still below the 2007 level.

The market eventually recovered, with the average reaching **£172,497 in 2015**.

It increased further to **£229,502 in 2020** and **£279,446 in 2025**.

Across the entire period, nominal appreciation was **285.68%**.

The equivalent compound annual growth rate was **5.55%**.

This annualised figure summarises the difference between the starting and ending averages. It does not imply that prices increased at the same rate each year.

The graph shows why long-term performance and short-term fluctuations should be considered together.

The second graph shows annual percentage changes in average recorded sale prices.

The strongest increase occurred in **2003**, when the average rose by **22.77%**.

In 2001, Nottingham recorded growth of **12.13%**.

The increase accelerated to **22.16% in 2002** and **22.77% in 2003**.

These early years contributed heavily to Nottingham's overall appreciation between 2000 and 2025.

However, changes in annual average transaction prices are not identical to changes in the underlying value of individual homes.

If more expensive properties account for a larger proportion of sales in a particular year, the average recorded price can rise even without an equivalent increase for every property.

This limitation is important when interpreting rapid annual growth.

## What happened during the 2008 financial crisis?

Nottingham's average recorded sale price reached **£154,364 in 2007**.

In 2008, it declined by **4.53%**, falling to **£147,366**.

This was the largest annual decline in the 2000–2025 dataset.

The maximum observed drawdown was also **4.53%**, from the 2007 peak to the 2008 trough.

Interestingly, the average then increased by **1.79% in 2009** and **1.99% in 2010**.

However, these increases were insufficient to restore the previous peak.

The average declined again by **1.40% in 2011**.

After further modest growth in 2012 and 2013, Nottingham finally exceeded its previous nominal peak in **2014**.

This illustrates the distinction between recording positive annual growth and recovering fully from an earlier downturn.

Investors who buy near a market peak may need to hold their properties for several years before the citywide average exceeds that level again.

## How many years did Nottingham house prices fall?

Across the 25 annual changes between 2001 and 2025, Nottingham recorded **23 positive years** and **2 negative years**.

The negative years were **2008 and 2011**.

The weakest year was **2008**, with a decline of **4.53%**.

The relatively small number of negative years does not mean the market was risk-free.

An individual property can perform differently from the citywide average.

Moreover, a property owner may face mortgage costs, repairs, void periods and other expenses even when nominal house prices are increasing.

Long-term appreciation should therefore not be confused with a guaranteed financial return.

## How much did Nottingham house prices rise between 2015 and 2025?

The average recorded sale price increased from **£172,497 in 2015** to **£279,446 in 2025**.

That represents **62.00% nominal growth** over ten years.

In 2016, Nottingham recorded annual growth of **5.32%**.

This was followed by **6.55% in 2017** and **3.99% in 2018**.

The annual increase slowed to **2.30% in 2019**.

Growth then accelerated considerably in 2020.

This sequence demonstrates why a ten-year growth percentage does not tell the whole story.

The path of prices can matter to investors who need to refinance, sell or release equity at a particular point.

## What happened to Nottingham house prices between 2020 and 2025?

Nottingham's average recorded sale price increased from **£229,502 in 2020** to **£279,446 in 2025**.

That represents **21.76% nominal growth** over five years.

In 2020 itself, the annual increase was **11.44%**.

The average then increased by **10.04% in 2021**.

Growth slowed to **5.04% in 2022** and **2.20% in 2023**.

It moderated further to **1.14% in 2024**.

In 2025, growth increased slightly to **1.91%**.

Unlike some markets that recorded a negative annual change in this period, Nottingham's average continued to rise in every year from 2020 through 2025.

Nevertheless, the rate of appreciation was substantially lower in 2025 than in 2020 or 2021.

A slowdown in the rate of growth is not the same as a decline in prices.

The dataset also cannot establish the causes of these movements or predict future price changes.

## How many properties were sold in Nottingham?

The third graph shows annual recorded property transactions.

Across 2000–2025, the dataset contains **304,358 Nottingham transactions**.

That is an average of approximately **11,706 sales per year**.

The highest annual total was **16,672 in 2007**.

The lowest was **8,231 in 2004**.

The lowest recorded transaction count occurred in 2004, a year when average prices were still increasing rapidly.

This is a useful reminder that annual transaction volumes and price growth do not necessarily move together.

Sales counts provide evidence about completed transactions, but they do not directly measure the number of buyers, available properties or rental demand.

## How did Nottingham property transactions change after 2007?

Nottingham recorded **16,672 transactions in 2007**.

The count fell sharply to **8,605 in 2008**.

In 2009, the total was **8,565**.

Recorded sales remained relatively subdued at **8,795 in 2010**.

By 2015, transaction activity had recovered to **12,039 sales**.

The dataset records **9,892 sales in 2020** and **10,906 in 2025**.

These changes help explain why property prices and transaction volumes should be examined together.

The average recorded sale price depends partly on which properties were sold in a particular year.

A change in the number or mix of transactions can influence the annual average.

This makes it especially important not to interpret an average-price series as a constant-quality valuation index.

## How volatile have Nottingham house prices been?

The sample standard deviation of Nottingham's annual growth rates was **6.55 percentage points**.

This measures the historical variation in annual average-price growth.

The maximum observed drawdown was **-4.53%**.

These figures describe historical movements rather than forecasting future volatility.

They also exclude risks associated with financing and operating an investment property.

For example, mortgage interest payments may rise even when rental income remains unchanged.

Maintenance costs, void periods, insurance and taxation can also affect returns.

An investor therefore needs to consider net cash flow alongside potential capital appreciation.

## Is Nottingham a good place to invest in property?

Nottingham experienced substantial nominal house-price appreciation between 2000 and 2025.

However, this does not establish whether an individual property is a good investment today.

Investors should examine the asking price, local rental market, property condition, financing arrangements and likely operating expenses.

Different Nottingham neighbourhoods and property types may produce substantially different outcomes.

**Our dataset contains sale prices and transaction counts, not rental income.**

It cannot calculate rental yields or identify the most profitable postcodes.

Nor can it predict future capital growth.

Historical data is useful for understanding market behaviour, but it should be combined with property-specific financial analysis.

## What can property investors learn from Nottingham's history?

Three findings are particularly relevant.

First, Nottingham's average recorded sale price increased by **285.68%** between 2000 and 2025.

Second, the financial-crisis downturn was followed by a prolonged recovery, with the previous nominal peak not exceeded until **2014**.

Third, price growth accelerated strongly in 2020 and 2021 before moderating over subsequent years.

These patterns show that the timing of a purchase can influence the capital appreciation experienced over a particular holding period.

They also reinforce the importance of evaluating cash flow independently from historical capital growth.

## Data source and methodology

This article uses **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, grouped by the Town/City label **NOTTINGHAM**, for 2000–2025 inclusive.

Annual average prices are arithmetic means of recorded sale prices.

Annual growth is calculated as the percentage change between consecutive annual averages.

Cumulative growth compares the 2000 and 2025 averages.

The compound annual growth rate uses the 25 elapsed years between those dates.

Annual growth volatility is calculated using the sample standard deviation of the 25 annual percentage changes.

Maximum drawdown measures the largest percentage decline from a preceding annual-average peak.

Transaction counts represent recorded completed sales.

**Important limitations:** All prices are nominal and have not been adjusted for inflation. Changes in the mix of properties sold can affect annual averages. Town/City labels do not necessarily correspond to local-authority boundaries.

This analysis is not a constant-quality house-price index. It excludes rental income, financing costs, maintenance and taxation.

Historical performance does not guarantee future investment returns.
