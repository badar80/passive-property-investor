Liverpool and Bristol occupy very different positions in the UK housing market. Bristol has consistently commanded much higher average sale prices, but has that translated into stronger percentage growth?

Using **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, we analysed annual average recorded sale prices between 2000 and 2025, covering 26 calendar years.

The results reveal a striking similarity: **Liverpool's average increased by 290.48%**, while **Bristol's increased by 285.99%**.

Yet the financial implications for a prospective property buyer are quite different, particularly when comparing purchase prices and the cash needed for a deposit.

## Liverpool vs Bristol: key statistics

| Measure | Liverpool | Bristol |

| Average price, 2015 | £151,978 | £261,671 |
| Average price, 2020 | £185,832 | £342,336 |
| Average price, 2025 | £239,652 | £398,183 |
| Growth, 2000–2025 | 290.48% | 285.99% |
| Growth, 2015–2025 | 57.69% | 52.17% |

The figures represent arithmetic averages of recorded transactions, not valuations of an unchanged set of properties.

## How have average house prices changed?

<img src="/images/article-42/liverpool-vs-bristol-average-house-prices.png" alt="Line chart comparing average recorded sale prices in Liverpool and Bristol from 2000 to 2025" loading="lazy" />

In 2000, Liverpool's average recorded sale price was **£61,374**, compared with **£103,158 in Bristol**.

By 2025, the averages had increased to **£239,652 in Liverpool** and **£398,183 in Bristol**.

Bristol was more expensive in all 26 years.

The absolute difference between the two cities increased from **£41,784** in 2000 to **£158,531** in 2025.

This illustrates an important distinction: similar percentage appreciation does not produce similar increases in pounds when starting prices differ.

A more expensive property can experience a much larger nominal gain even when its percentage growth rate is almost identical to that of a cheaper property.

However, the annual averages may be influenced by the types of homes sold in each year, so they should not be interpreted as direct valuations of individual properties.

## Which city achieved stronger percentage growth?

<img src="/images/article-42/liverpool-vs-bristol-indexed-growth.png" alt="Indexed average house-price growth in Liverpool and Bristol with both cities starting at 100 in 2000" loading="lazy" />

To compare growth fairly in percentage terms, we indexed both cities to **100 in 2000**.

By 2025, Liverpool's index had reached **390.5**, compared with **386.0 for Bristol**.

Liverpool's cumulative nominal growth was **290.48%**, exceeding Bristol's **285.99%** by just **4.49 percentage points**.

Their compound annual growth rates were similarly close: **5.60% for Liverpool** and **5.55% for Bristol**.

Over the full 25-year interval, the two markets therefore delivered remarkably similar percentage changes in average recorded sale prices.

This does not mean that individual properties in the two cities generated equivalent investment returns.

The calculation excludes rental income, mortgage costs, maintenance, tax, transaction costs and inflation.

## Did Liverpool or Bristol grow faster after 2015?

Over 2015–2025, Liverpool's average increased from **£151,978** to **£239,652**, representing **57.69%** growth.

Bristol increased from **£261,671** to **£398,183**, representing **52.17%** growth.

Liverpool therefore outperformed Bristol by **5.52 percentage points** over this decade.

The difference is more noticeable than the narrow gap over the full 2000–2025 period.

This demonstrates how the selected timeframe can change the interpretation of historical market performance.

Investors comparing cities should consider several periods rather than relying on a single starting year, particularly where property cycles and mortgage conditions have changed substantially.

## How has Bristol's price premium changed?

<img src="/images/article-42/bristol-price-premium-over-liverpool.png" alt="Bristol average house-price premium over Liverpool from 2000 to 2025" loading="lazy" />

The third graph measures how much more expensive Bristol was than Liverpool, expressed as a percentage of Liverpool's average price.

In 2000, Bristol's premium was **68.08%**.

In 2015, it stood at **72.18%**, rising to **84.22%** in 2020.

By 2025, Bristol's premium was **66.15%**.

Across the full period, the percentage premium was therefore remarkably similar at the beginning and end, despite substantial fluctuations in between.

Its highest observed value was **94.19% in 2018**, while its lowest was **40.95% in 2009**.

The pound difference tells another story. Bristol's average exceeded Liverpool's by **£41,784** in 2000 and **£158,531** in 2025.

This is why percentage premiums and absolute price gaps should be examined together.

## Which city's house prices were more volatile?

We measured the sample standard deviation of annual percentage changes in average recorded sale prices.

Liverpool's annual growth volatility was **7.23 percentage points**.

Bristol's was **6.09 percentage points**.

Bristol therefore exhibited lower year-to-year variation in its annual growth figures.

However, this does not necessarily imply a smaller risk of loss during a market downturn.

Volatility measures variation across annual changes, while maximum drawdown measures the largest cumulative fall from an earlier peak.

Both indicators are historical summaries and do not represent the risk profile of any individual property.

## What happened during the financial crisis?

Liverpool's largest observed drawdown was **-4.61%**, from its **2007** annual-average peak to its **2011** trough.

Its average recovered to the previous nominal peak in **2014**.

Bristol's largest drawdown was deeper, at **-8.79%**, from **2007 to 2009**.

Bristol recovered its previous nominal annual-average peak in **2013**.

The contrast is interesting: Bristol experienced a larger observed decline but recovered earlier, while Liverpool's decline was smaller but lasted longer.

For landlords using mortgage finance, the duration of a downturn can matter just as much as the eventual price recovery.

Refinancing requirements, interest costs, rental voids and liquidity can all influence whether an investment remains sustainable during weaker market conditions.

## Do Liverpool and Bristol prices move together?

The correlation between the cities' annual percentage price changes was **+0.512**.

This represents a moderate positive historical relationship, but it is far from a perfect correlation.

Both markets may respond to nationwide influences such as borrowing costs, inflation and changes in mortgage availability.

However, local housing stock, buyer demand and transaction patterns can cause their annual averages to behave differently.

Correlation does not establish causation, and a historical relationship is not a reliable forecast of future prices.

## Liverpool vs Bristol: which is better for property investment?

Liverpool's lower average recorded sale price could make it more accessible to investors working with a limited deposit.

For illustration, a 25% deposit on the 2025 citywide average would be approximately **£59,913 in Liverpool**, compared with **£99,546 in Bristol**.

This is only a mathematical illustration, not a mortgage quotation or a comparison of equivalent properties.

Bristol's higher average prices may reflect differences in its housing market, but price alone cannot establish better rental demand or stronger investment performance.

Our dataset includes recorded sale prices and transaction counts. It does **not** contain achievable rents, yields, running costs or net cash flow.

Consequently, it cannot determine which city offers the better buy-to-let investment return.

An investor should examine the specific postcode, property type, local rents, mortgage terms, maintenance obligations, taxes and realistic vacancy assumptions.

A cheaper property is not automatically a better investment, just as a more expensive property is not automatically a safer one.

## Data source and methodology

This analysis uses **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, grouped by the Town/City labels **LIVERPOOL** and **BRISTOL**, covering 2000–2025 inclusive.

Annual average prices are arithmetic means of recorded transactions. Cumulative growth compares the averages at the beginning and end of the period.

Compound annual growth rates use the 25 elapsed years between 2000 and 2025.

Annual growth volatility is the sample standard deviation of annual percentage changes. Maximum drawdown is the largest percentage decline from a previous annual-average peak.

Bristol's premium is calculated as Bristol's annual average divided by Liverpool's annual average, minus one.

**Important limitations:** These are nominal, not inflation-adjusted figures. The dataset is not a constant-quality house-price index. Changes in the mix of properties sold can influence averages, and Town/City labels do not necessarily correspond to administrative boundaries.

Historical growth is not a prediction of future property returns.
