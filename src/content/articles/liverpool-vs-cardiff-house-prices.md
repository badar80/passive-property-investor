Liverpool and Cardiff are two major British cities with distinct housing markets. Both experienced considerable house-price growth between 2000 and 2025, but their average prices and patterns of appreciation differed.

Using **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, we analysed 26 years of annual average recorded sale prices, comparing cumulative growth, price gaps, volatility and recovery from downturns.

**Liverpool achieved 290.48% nominal growth between 2000 and 2025**, compared with **276.90% for Cardiff**.

Yet Cardiff remained substantially more expensive throughout the entire period.

## How have Liverpool and Cardiff house prices changed?

<img src="/images/article-41/liverpool-vs-cardiff-average-house-prices.png" alt="Average recorded house sale prices in Liverpool and Cardiff from 2000 to 2025" loading="lazy" />

In 2000, Liverpool's average recorded sale price was **£61,374**, while Cardiff's was **£85,626**.

By 2025, Liverpool's average had reached **£239,652** and Cardiff's had risen to **£322,724**.

Cardiff was more expensive in every year of the comparison.

The graph shows that both cities experienced substantial long-term appreciation, interrupted by periods of slower growth and temporary declines.

However, annual averages can be affected by changes in the mix of homes sold. A year containing a higher proportion of expensive detached houses can produce a higher average without an equivalent increase in the value of individual properties.

This is why the figures should not be treated as constant-quality house-price indices.

## Which city delivered stronger capital growth?

<img src="/images/article-41/liverpool-vs-cardiff-indexed-growth.png" alt="Indexed house-price growth in Liverpool and Cardiff with both cities starting at 100 in 2000" loading="lazy" />

Cardiff began the period at a substantially higher average price, so comparing the absolute pound values alone does not reveal which market grew faster.

We therefore indexed both cities to **100 in 2000**.

By 2025, Liverpool's index reached **390.5**, compared with **376.9** for Cardiff.

Liverpool's cumulative growth of **290.48%** exceeded Cardiff's **276.90%** by **13.58 percentage points**.

The compound annual growth rates were **5.60% for Liverpool** and **5.45% for Cardiff**.

Liverpool therefore recorded stronger historical percentage appreciation across the full 25-year interval.

These are nominal changes in annual transaction averages. They do not account for inflation, rental income, mortgage interest, tax or other investment costs.

## What happened between 2015 and 2025?

Over the latest decade in our dataset, Liverpool's average increased from **£151,978** to **£239,652**.

That represents **57.69% growth**.

Cardiff's average rose from **£205,121** to **£322,724**, equivalent to **57.33%**.

The difference was just **0.36 percentage points** in Liverpool's favour.

This is a much narrower gap than the difference in growth over the entire 2000–2025 period.

It demonstrates why selecting a time horizon matters. A city can outperform over 25 years while showing almost identical percentage growth to another city over the most recent decade.

Historical performance also cannot establish which market will appreciate more rapidly in the future.

## Why is Cardiff still substantially more expensive?

<img src="/images/article-41/cardiff-price-premium-over-liverpool.png" alt="Cardiff average house-price premium over Liverpool from 2000 to 2025" loading="lazy" />

The third graph measures Cardiff's average-price premium over Liverpool as a percentage.

In 2000, Cardiff's average was **39.52% higher**, representing a difference of **£24,253**.

In 2015, its premium stood at **34.97%**.

By 2025, the premium had narrowed to **34.66%**, but the absolute price gap had grown to **£83,071**.

The premium was not consistently falling. It reached **60.55% in 2002**, while its lowest recorded level was **23.97% in 2009**.

This fluctuation is important: the relationship between two property markets rarely follows a straight line.

A narrowing percentage premium does not necessarily mean the pound difference between their average prices is shrinking.

For buyers, the actual purchase price and deposit required may matter more than the percentage gap alone.

## Which city experienced greater price volatility?

We calculated the annual percentage change in average recorded sale prices for each city, then measured the sample standard deviation of those changes.

Liverpool's annual growth volatility was **7.23 percentage points**.

Cardiff's was **6.24 percentage points**.

Cardiff therefore exhibited less year-to-year variation in its annual percentage growth figures.

However, this did not mean Cardiff avoided substantial downturns.

Its maximum observed drawdown was **-5.76%**, compared with **-4.61% for Liverpool**.

Volatility and drawdown measure different things. A market can experience relatively consistent annual movements while still suffering a significant cumulative decline.

Neither measure represents the maximum possible loss on an individual property.

## How did the financial crisis affect Liverpool and Cardiff?

Liverpool's largest observed decline ran from its **2007** annual-average peak to its **2011** trough.

Its annual average returned to the previous nominal peak in **2014**.

Cardiff's largest decline occurred between **2007 and 2009**, with recovery in **2013**.

Cardiff experienced the deeper recorded drawdown but recovered its previous annual-average peak earlier.

This illustrates why investors should examine both the magnitude and duration of a downturn.

For a leveraged landlord, refinancing requirements, mortgage interest rates, rental voids and cash reserves can be more important than the eventual recovery in a citywide average.

These drawdowns are based on annual transaction averages, not continuous property valuations.

## Do Liverpool and Cardiff house prices move together?

The Pearson correlation between annual percentage changes in the two cities was **+0.703**.

This indicates a positive historical relationship between their annual price movements.

Broad economic influences, including credit conditions and borrowing costs, may affect both markets. However, correlation does not establish causation.

Local housing stock, employment conditions, transaction volumes and buyer demand can also produce different results in individual years.

A historical correlation should not be interpreted as a forecast of future price movements.

## Liverpool or Cardiff: which is better for property investment?

On historical percentage capital growth, Liverpool performed slightly better over the full period and almost identically to Cardiff over 2015–2025.

Liverpool also had a considerably lower average recorded sale price in 2025, potentially reducing the cash required for a purchase at the same loan-to-value ratio.

Cardiff, meanwhile, exhibited lower annual growth volatility and recovered its previous nominal average-price peak earlier after the financial crisis.

But **house-price appreciation alone does not determine investment profitability**.

A buy-to-let investor also needs to examine rental demand, achievable monthly rent, mortgage costs, maintenance, insurance, management fees, taxes and possible void periods.

A particular Cardiff flat or Liverpool terrace can perform very differently from its citywide average.

Our dataset contains sale prices and transaction counts, not rental income or property operating costs. It therefore cannot establish which city offers better net rental yields or total investment returns.

For an investment decision, postcode-level research and a realistic property-specific cash-flow model are essential.

## Data source and methodology

This analysis uses **HM Land Registry Price Paid Data** grouped by the Town/City labels **LIVERPOOL** and **CARDIFF**, covering 2000–2025 inclusive.

Annual average prices are arithmetic means of recorded sale prices. Cumulative growth compares the annual averages at the beginning and end of the selected period.

Compound annual growth rates use the number of elapsed years. Volatility is the sample standard deviation of annual percentage changes. Maximum drawdown measures the largest fall from a previous annual-average peak.

The Cardiff premium is calculated as Cardiff's annual average divided by Liverpool's annual average, minus one, expressed as a percentage.

**Limitations:** Prices are nominal and not inflation-adjusted. Changes in the composition of properties sold can influence annual averages. Town/City labels do not necessarily match administrative boundaries. The data is not a constant-quality price index.

Past performance does not predict future property returns.
