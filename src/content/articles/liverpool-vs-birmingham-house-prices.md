Liverpool and Birmingham are two of England's major urban property markets. Both experienced substantial house-price growth between 2000 and 2025, but which delivered stronger historical capital appreciation?

We analysed **26 years of HM Land Registry Price Paid Data**, comparing annual average recorded sale prices, percentage growth, volatility and the changing price gap between the cities.

**Liverpool recorded 290.48% nominal growth between 2000 and 2025**, compared with **268.69% for Birmingham**.

Despite Liverpool's stronger percentage growth, Birmingham had the higher average recorded sale price in almost every year.

These are annual arithmetic means of recorded transactions, not valuations of identical properties.

## How have Liverpool and Birmingham house prices changed?

<img src="/images/article-39/liverpool-vs-birmingham-average-house-prices.png" alt="Liverpool and Birmingham average recorded house sale prices from 2000 to 2025" loading="lazy" />

In 2000, Liverpool's average recorded sale price was **£61,374**, compared with **£70,790 in Birmingham**.

By 2025, Liverpool's average had reached **£239,652**, while Birmingham's stood at **£261,000**.

Birmingham was more expensive in **25 of 26 years**, while Liverpool was more expensive in **1 year**.

The chart reveals substantial appreciation in both cities, interrupted by periods of slower growth and declining annual averages.

However, these figures reflect the properties actually sold in each year. Changes in the mix of flats, terraces, detached homes and neighbourhoods can influence the averages independently of underlying property values.

## Which city achieved stronger long-term growth?

<img src="/images/article-39/liverpool-vs-birmingham-indexed-growth.png" alt="Indexed Liverpool and Birmingham average house-price growth with 2000 equal to 100" loading="lazy" />

Comparing absolute house prices can be misleading because Birmingham started the period at a higher average price.

The second chart therefore sets both cities' average recorded prices to **100 in 2000**, making their relative percentage growth easier to compare.

By 2025, Liverpool's index reached **390.5**, compared with **368.7** for Birmingham.

Liverpool achieved **290.48% cumulative growth**, exceeding Birmingham's **268.69%**.

The corresponding compound annual growth rates were **5.60% for Liverpool** and **5.36% for Birmingham**.

Although the annualised difference looks modest, compounding over 25 years creates a noticeable difference in total percentage appreciation.

These are historical nominal growth figures, not investment returns after costs or inflation.

## What happened between 2015 and 2025?

Liverpool's average recorded sale price increased from **£151,978** in 2015 to **£239,652** in 2025.

That represents **57.69% growth** over the decade.

Birmingham's average rose from **£169,448** to **£261,000**, equivalent to **54.03%**.

Liverpool therefore outperformed Birmingham on this measure over both the full 25-year period and the latest decade.

Nevertheless, growth rates depend on the starting and ending years selected. Investors should investigate shorter periods, individual neighbourhoods and different property types before making decisions.

A strong historical growth record is not evidence that the same city will outperform in the future.

## When did Liverpool become more expensive than Birmingham?

<img src="/images/article-39/birmingham-price-premium-over-liverpool.png" alt="Birmingham average house-price premium over Liverpool from 2000 to 2025, highlighting the 2009 crossover" loading="lazy" />

The third graph measures Birmingham's annual average-price premium over Liverpool as a percentage.

In 2000, Birmingham was **15.34% more expensive**, a difference of **£9,417**.

But something unusual happened in **2009**.

Liverpool's average recorded sale price was **£141,521**, while Birmingham's was **£141,459**.

Liverpool was therefore approximately **£62 more expensive** that year, giving Birmingham a premium of **-0.04%**.

This was the only annual crossover in the dataset. The difference was extremely small and should not be interpreted as proof that otherwise comparable Liverpool homes were more valuable.

By 2025, Birmingham had regained a premium of **8.91%**, equivalent to **£21,347**.

The percentage premium narrowed between 2000 and 2025, even though the absolute difference in pounds increased.

## Which city experienced more volatile price growth?

We calculated the sample standard deviation of annual percentage changes in average recorded sale prices.

Liverpool's annual growth volatility was **7.23 percentage points**, compared with **6.71 percentage points** for Birmingham.

Liverpool therefore experienced greater year-to-year variation in percentage growth.

However, Birmingham suffered a larger maximum observed drawdown.

Liverpool's largest decline from a preceding annual-average peak was **-4.61%**, compared with **-8.64%** for Birmingham.

These measures capture different aspects of market behaviour. A city with lower annual volatility can still experience a deeper cumulative fall during a particular downturn.

Neither statistic measures the maximum potential loss on an individual property.

## How did the cities recover after the financial crisis?

Liverpool's largest observed drawdown ran from **2007 to 2011**.

Its earlier nominal annual-average price peak was regained in **2014**.

Birmingham's largest observed decline ran from **2007 to 2009**, with recovery to the preceding peak in **2014**.

Both cities recovered their previous nominal annual-average peaks in 2014, although Birmingham's recorded decline was deeper.

For leveraged property investors, a period of declining prices can be particularly challenging if a mortgage product expires, refinancing becomes more expensive or a property must be sold unexpectedly.

Maintaining cash reserves and sustainable borrowing arrangements matters regardless of the long-term direction of house prices.

## Do Liverpool and Birmingham house prices move together?

The Pearson correlation between annual percentage changes in the two cities was **+0.708**.

This indicates a positive historical association between their annual price movements.

Nevertheless, the relationship is far from perfect. Differences in local housing stock, transaction activity and market conditions can produce divergent annual results.

Correlation does not establish that changes in one city's property prices cause changes in the other.

It also does not guarantee that the markets will behave similarly during future economic shocks.

## Liverpool or Birmingham: which is better for property investment?

**Liverpool leads on historical percentage capital growth** over both 2000–2025 and 2015–2025.

It also had a lower average recorded sale price in 2025, potentially reducing the absolute deposit required at a given loan-to-value ratio.

However, historical capital appreciation is only one component of investment performance.

Buy-to-let investors must also consider achievable rents, mortgage interest, maintenance, insurance, management fees, voids, tax and the costs of buying and selling.

Birmingham and Liverpool contain varied neighbourhoods and property types. A well-selected property in either city may perform very differently from its citywide average.

The data used here does not contain rental income or operating costs, so it cannot establish which city offers better net rental yields or overall investment returns.

A more useful next step for an investor is to compare specific postcodes, property types and realistic cash-flow scenarios.

## Data source and methodology

- **Source:** HM Land Registry Price Paid Data.
- **Geography:** Liverpool and Birmingham Town/City labels.
- **Period:** 2000–2025 inclusive.
- **Average price:** Arithmetic mean of recorded sale prices for each year.
- **Total growth:** Percentage change between annual-average endpoint prices.
- **CAGR:** Compound annual growth rate over elapsed years.
- **Volatility:** Sample standard deviation of annual percentage growth rates.
- **Maximum drawdown:** Largest decline from a preceding annual-average peak.
- **Birmingham premium:** Birmingham average divided by Liverpool average, minus one, expressed as a percentage.

Prices are **nominal and not inflation-adjusted**. These are not constant-quality house-price indices, and the mix of properties sold can influence annual averages.

Town/City labels do not necessarily correspond to local-authority boundaries. Past performance does not predict future returns.
