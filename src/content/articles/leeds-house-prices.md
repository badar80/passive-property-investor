## Leeds House Prices: 2000–2025 Data Analysis

Explore Leeds average sold house prices, annual changes and long-term growth from 2000 to 2025. This analysis uses annual district-level transaction counts and arithmetic mean prices from the supplied filtered Land Registry export.

## Key findings

In **Leeds**, the mean recorded sale price was **£77,237 in 2000** and **£289,999 in 2025**. That is a **275.5%** change across the period. The 2025 annual mean was **+2.9%** relative to 2024. There were **10,337** qualifying transactions in the 2025 extract.

## Understanding Leeds property market cycles

Leeds recorded substantial growth in average sold prices between 2000 and 2025, but the annual figures reveal a more complicated history than the headline increase suggests.

The dataset shows rapid growth before 2007, a decline during the financial crisis, an uneven recovery and further increases during the 2020s.

The figures represent arithmetic mean prices for qualifying transactions in each year. They are not a repeat-sales index measuring the appreciation of identical properties.

### 2000–2007: Rapid increases before the financial crisis

Leeds' average recorded sold price increased from £77,237 in 2000 to £170,659 in 2007.

That represents growth of approximately +121.0% over seven years.

The number of qualifying sales increased from 14,961 in 2000 to 17,775 in 2007.

The highest transaction count during this period was 19,286 in 2006.

The combination of higher average prices and substantial transaction activity indicates a period of significant market expansion.

However, the arithmetic mean can also be affected by changes in the types and locations of properties being sold.

### 2008–2009: Prices and transactions decline

The average recorded price fell from £170,659 in 2007 to £166,468 in 2008, followed by £158,931 in 2009.

The cumulative change between 2007 and 2009 was approximately -6.9%.

Transaction activity fell more sharply. The dataset records 17,775 qualifying sales in 2007 but only 8,172 in 2009.

That represents a reduction of approximately 54.0% in recorded transactions.

This distinction matters when interpreting historical housing data: a relatively modest change in average prices can coincide with a much larger contraction in the number of sales.

### 2010–2013: An uneven recovery

The average recorded price recovered to £172,055 in 2010, exceeding the 2007 figure.

However, this was not the beginning of an uninterrupted rise.

The average declined to £166,443 in 2011, before reaching £169,386 in 2012 and £172,883 in 2013.

Between 2010 and 2013, the change was only +0.5%.

This period illustrates why selecting a single start and end year can obscure the fluctuations that occur between them.

For property investors, a historical growth percentage is more informative when considered alongside the intervening years and the prevailing transaction volumes.

### 2014–2019: Gradual and sustained price increases

The average recorded sold price increased from £178,191 in 2014 to £218,951 in 2019.

That represents growth of approximately +22.9%.

Unlike the sharp movements recorded during some earlier years, this period shows a sequence of annual increases in the average price.

Transaction counts remained relatively consistent compared with the extreme changes around 2007–2009.

There were 12,070 qualifying transactions in 2014 and 11,764 in 2019.

Nevertheless, these are district-wide averages. Different property types and individual neighbourhoods may have experienced different patterns.

### 2020–2022: Another period of strong growth

The average recorded price increased from £240,280 in 2020 to £271,685 in 2022.

That is a rise of approximately +13.1% over two years.

Transaction activity also changed, with 10,165 qualifying sales in 2020, 13,168 in 2021 and 11,102 in 2022.

The data establishes that average transaction prices increased during this period. It does not isolate the effects of mortgage conditions, inflation, buyer preferences or changes in the composition of properties sold.

### 2023–2025: A pause followed by further increases

One particularly interesting feature of the Leeds dataset is the movement between 2022 and 2023.

The mean recorded price was £271,685 in 2022 and £271,612 in 2023.

This was a difference of just £73, representing approximately -0.0%.

The average subsequently increased to £281,749 in 2024 and £289,999 in 2025.

The 2025 average was approximately +2.9% above 2024.

Recorded sales increased from 9,309 in 2023 to 10,337 in 2025, although the latest year's figures may be revised as further transactions are registered.

## What the Leeds figures mean for property investors

### Long-term price growth is not the same as investment profit

The increase from £77,237 to £289,999 represents approximately +275.5% in nominal average recorded prices.

This is not the net return earned by a landlord who purchased a property in 2000.

An actual investment calculation would need to account for purchase costs, mortgage interest, repairs, refurbishment, management fees, taxation and selling costs.

Rental income would also need to be included when calculating total investment returns.

The historical prices in this article are not adjusted for inflation.

### Transaction volumes add important context

Leeds recorded 19,286 qualifying sales in 2006, compared with 7,862 in 2011.

When transaction counts change substantially, the composition of properties sold can also change.

For example, a greater proportion of detached properties in one year's sales could increase the arithmetic mean even without equivalent growth in every property category.

This is why the mean should be interpreted alongside transaction volumes rather than in isolation.

### Property-level research is still essential

The figures cover Leeds at the district-name level used in the supplied Land Registry extract.

They cannot establish the rental yield, capital appreciation or investment suitability of a particular street or property.

A buyer would still need comparable local sales, rental evidence, a realistic estimate of running costs and an assessment of the building's condition.

Historical averages are useful for identifying broad patterns, but they are not a substitute for local due diligence.

## Methodology and limitations

- Source: user-supplied annual export derived from HM Land Registry Price Paid transactions.
- Geographic level: Land Registry district names as recorded in the export, not local authority boundary-normalised comparisons.
- Years: calendar years 2000 2025 inclusive.
- Filters applied when producing the supplied extract: `PPDCategoryType = A`, `PropertyType IN (D, S, T, F)`, with mean sale price calculated across included transactions.
- Average prices are arithmetic means, not medians or repeat-sales indices; no inflation adjustment.
- 2025 data may be affected by registration lag or later revisions.
- This is descriptive historical analysis, not an investment forecast or valuation.
